COBRA vs. Marketplace Insurance After Losing Your Job

Marcus Reyes Insurance & Risk Writer Updated Aug 3, 2026 8 min read

Losing a job comes with a stack of decisions that all need answers within a couple of weeks, and health insurance tends to be the one people put off the longest — usually because it feels the most complicated. It doesn't have to be. Once you understand what COBRA actually is and how it stacks up against a marketplace plan, the decision usually becomes pretty clear within about ten minutes.

What COBRA actually is

COBRA (Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act) gives you the right to keep your exact same employer health plan after you leave a job — same doctors, same network, same prescription coverage — for up to 18 months in most cases, sometimes longer for specific qualifying events. The catch is cost: your employer was almost certainly covering a meaningful chunk of your premium while you were employed, and under COBRA, you pay the entire premium yourself, plus up to a 2% administrative fee.

Why COBRA often shocks people: if your paycheck showed a $150/month deduction for health insurance while employed, your actual COBRA premium is frequently $600–$700+ a month, because that $150 was only your share — your employer was quietly covering the rest. Seeing the full premium for the first time is where most people's COBRA sticker shock comes from.

What a marketplace plan offers instead

ACA marketplace plans are priced independently of your former employer, and — critically — losing job-based coverage qualifies you for a Special Enrollment Period, typically 60 days, to sign up outside the normal fall open enrollment window. Depending on your income for the year (which may be lower than usual right after a layoff), you may qualify for meaningful subsidies that bring your monthly premium down significantly, sometimes below what you'd pay for COBRA on the exact same tier of coverage.

When COBRA is genuinely the better choice

  • You're mid-treatment for something — chemotherapy, a surgery recovery, an ongoing specialist relationship — where switching networks or restarting a deductible mid-course would be disruptive or costly.
  • You've already met your deductible or out-of-pocket max for the year under your old plan. Switching to a marketplace plan usually means starting that count over from zero, which can cost more overall even if the monthly premium is lower.
  • Your income this year is high enough that you wouldn't qualify for meaningful marketplace subsidies anyway — for example, if you received a large severance package that counts as income.

When the marketplace usually wins

  • Your income has dropped meaningfully due to the job loss, which often unlocks subsidies that can make marketplace premiums considerably cheaper than COBRA's full-price tag.
  • You're generally healthy and not mid-treatment for anything, so a new deductible year and a new network aren't a real disruption.
  • You expect the job search to take a while and want a plan you can keep long-term rather than one with an 18-month clock already ticking.

A detail that trips people up: you can switch later

Electing COBRA doesn't lock you in for the full 18 months. You can generally drop COBRA and switch to a marketplace plan later, either during the annual open enrollment period or if you experience another qualifying event. Some people intentionally use COBRA as a short-term bridge — for instance, to finish out a course of treatment — and then move to a marketplace plan once that's resolved. Just be aware that switching away from COBRA outside of open enrollment isn't automatically its own special enrollment trigger in every case, so check the specific rules before assuming you can switch whenever you want.

The decision usually comes down to one question: is there something about your current plan — a doctor, a deductible you've already paid into, an ongoing treatment — worth paying full price to keep? If yes, COBRA. If not, get a marketplace quote before assuming COBRA is your only option.

Practical steps for the first two weeks after a layoff

  1. Confirm your last day of employer coverage — it's not always your literal last day of work, sometimes it runs to the end of the month.
  2. Request a COBRA election notice from your former employer's HR or benefits administrator if it doesn't arrive automatically.
  3. Get a marketplace quote using your new, likely-lower estimated income for the year — do this even if you think you'll choose COBRA, just to see the real number.
  4. Check whether your doctors and any regular prescriptions are in-network on the marketplace plan you're considering.
  5. Make your decision within the enrollment window — both COBRA election and marketplace Special Enrollment typically give you 60 days, but don't let it slide to the deadline.

A layoff is stressful enough without adding a rushed insurance decision at the last minute. Getting both quotes side by side, even if you're fairly sure which way you'll go, takes less than an hour and can save real money either way.
