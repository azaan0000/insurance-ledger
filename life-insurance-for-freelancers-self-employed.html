Life Insurance for Freelancers and the Self-Employed

Marcus Reyes Insurance & Risk Writer Updated Jul 22, 2026 8 min read

When you work for someone else, life insurance often just... happens. HR enrolls you during onboarding, a small policy shows up as a line item on your benefits summary, and you never think about it again — until you leave the job and realize that policy walked out the door with you. Freelancers and self-employed workers don't get that default. There's no enrollment period, no automatic 1x-salary policy waiting in a benefits portal. You have to go build the whole thing yourself, from scratch, which is exactly why so many self-employed people simply... don't.

That gap is worth closing, and it's less complicated than it feels once you break it into pieces.

Why self-employment changes the calculation

Traditional employees who die unexpectedly leave behind a household that loses one income stream. Self-employed people often leave behind something more tangled: unfinished client contracts, business debt that may be personally guaranteed, equipment or software subscriptions still being paid off, and — if you have employees or contractors of your own — payroll obligations that don't stop just because you did. Your life insurance needs to cover your household and, in many cases, wind down or stabilize a business.

There's also an income-volatility problem. A salaried employee can point to a W-2 and get a fairly quick, fairly accurate coverage estimate. A freelancer's income might swing 40% year to year. Insurers know this, and it affects both how much you should buy and how insurers evaluate your application.

Working out your number as a self-employed person

Start with the same core framework anyone uses — debts, years of income replacement, future obligations like kids' education, minus existing savings — but add two freelancer-specific line items:

  • Business wind-down costs. If you disappeared tomorrow, what would it cost your family or a hired professional to close out client work, cancel contracts, and settle any business debts? For a solo consultant this might be a few thousand dollars; for someone running a small agency with staff, it could be tens of thousands.
  • Income volatility buffer. Rather than using your best year or your worst year as your baseline, average your last 3 years of net income and use that as your income-replacement figure. It smooths out the swings insurers and financial planners tend to worry about.
Real numbers: a freelance graphic designer averaging $75,000/year, with $15,000 in business debt, a $2,000 estimated wind-down cost, and a goal of replacing income for 12 years for a partner and two kids, would be looking at roughly $75,000 × 12 + $17,000 ≈ $917,000 in coverage before subtracting existing savings — noticeably more than a flat "10x salary" estimate of $750,000 would suggest.

Term life insurance, not a business-specific product

You don't need some exotic "freelancer insurance" product — for personal coverage, a standard individual term life policy works exactly the same way it would for a salaried employee. Where self-employment actually changes things is on the business side, if you have partners or co-owners: that's where a separate product called key person insurance or a buy-sell agreement funded by life insurance comes in, and it's worth a conversation with a business attorney or insurance broker if you're not a solo operator.

The underwriting hurdle freelancers should expect

Applying for life insurance without a W-2 usually means more paperwork, not more difficulty. Insurers typically want to see 1–2 years of tax returns (Schedule C or K-1 forms, depending on your business structure) to verify income, rather than the single pay stub a salaried applicant might provide. Have those documents ready before you apply — it's the single biggest thing that speeds up or slows down underwriting for self-employed applicants.

A few other things that tend to catch freelancers off guard during the application process:

  1. Income documentation takes longer. Build in extra time versus what a salaried friend might experience.
  2. Irregular income doesn't disqualify you — insurers average it, they don't penalize a single lean year as long as the overall trend is stable or growing.
  3. You may need an accountant's letter in some cases if your tax returns don't clearly show your income structure, particularly for newer business entities.

Group policies aren't entirely off the table

If you belong to a freelancers' union, professional association, or a chamber of commerce, check whether they offer group life insurance access. These plans are sometimes more expensive per dollar of coverage than an individually underwritten term policy for a healthy applicant, but they can be worth a look if you have a health condition that would make individual underwriting difficult, since group plans often have simplified or guaranteed-issue underwriting.

The absence of an HR department isn't a reason to skip life insurance — it's the reason you need to be more deliberate about buying it, since nobody's going to auto-enroll you.

Don't forget disability insurance while you're at it

This is a genuinely important point for self-employed people specifically: statistically, you're far more likely to become disabled and unable to work for an extended period than you are to die during your working years, and disability insurance is the product that actually covers that gap. Freelancers who buy life insurance but skip disability coverage have protected against the less likely risk while leaving the more likely one completely uncovered. If you're shopping for one, it's worth shopping for both at the same time.

A simple starting checklist

  • Gather 1–2 years of tax returns before you start requesting quotes
  • Calculate your coverage number using average income, not your best or worst year
  • Add a specific line item for business wind-down or debt, if applicable
  • Get quotes from at least 2–3 insurers, since self-employed underwriting can vary more between companies
  • Ask about disability insurance in the same conversation
  • Revisit your coverage amount any time your income shifts meaningfully or you take on business debt

Being your own boss means being your own HR department, your own benefits administrator, and your own safety net architect. Life insurance is one of the few pieces of that job you can knock out in an afternoon and then genuinely forget about for the next decade.
