Long-Term Care Insurance: When to Buy It and Why

Marcus Reyes Insurance & Risk Writer Updated Aug 7, 2026 9 min read

One of the more consequential misunderstandings in retirement planning is assuming Medicare will cover long-term care if it's ever needed. It largely doesn't. Medicare covers limited, short-term skilled nursing care following a hospital stay — not the extended custodial care many people eventually need, whether at home, in assisted living, or in a nursing facility. That gap is exactly what long-term care insurance is built to address, and it's a gap that catches a lot of families off guard at the worst possible time to be figuring it out.

What "long-term care" actually means

Long-term care refers to a range of services supporting people who can no longer independently perform basic daily activities — bathing, dressing, eating, mobility — due to a chronic illness, disability, or cognitive decline like dementia. This can happen at home with the help of a paid caregiver, in an assisted living facility, or in a skilled nursing facility, and the associated costs vary enormously depending on the setting and level of care needed.

The Medicare gap, explicitly: Medicare generally covers up to 100 days of skilled nursing care following a qualifying hospital stay, with significant cost-sharing after the first 20 days — and that's it. It does not cover extended custodial care, which is the type of ongoing help with daily activities that most long-term care situations actually involve. Medicaid can cover long-term care, but generally only after a person has spent down most of their assets to qualify, which is a very different outcome than most families would choose if given options in advance.

What long-term care actually costs

Costs vary enormously by region and type of care, but they're consistently among the largest unplanned expenses many families face later in life — a private room in a nursing facility, in-home care with meaningful daily hours, or assisted living can each run into significant five- or even six-figure annual costs depending on location and level of care needed. Multiply that by even a few years of care, which is common for conditions like dementia, and the total can meaningfully erode a lifetime of savings.

What long-term care insurance covers

A long-term care policy typically pays a daily or monthly benefit amount toward covered care costs, up to a maximum benefit period (a set number of years) or lifetime maximum, once you meet the policy's benefit trigger — commonly needing help with a specified number of "activities of daily living," or a cognitive impairment diagnosis. Policies vary considerably in their daily benefit amount, benefit period length, inflation protection, and elimination period (a waiting period before benefits begin, similar to a deductible in time rather than dollars).

Why timing the purchase matters so much

This is the single most important factor in long-term care insurance, more than almost any other detail. Premiums are priced based largely on your age and health at the time of purchase, and they rise substantially the older and less healthy you are when you apply. Beyond cost, insurability itself becomes a real issue — a diagnosis of a chronic condition, or even early signs of cognitive decline, can make you ineligible for coverage altogether, not just more expensive.

Age at purchaseGeneral pricing and eligibility pattern
Mid-50sOften considered a common sweet spot — healthy enough to qualify easily, premiums meaningfully lower than waiting
Early 60sStill generally available, but premiums notably higher than a decade earlier
Late 60s and beyondCoverage becomes harder to obtain, premiums rise substantially, and health-related denials become more common

The tradeoff nobody loves: it's a "use it or lose it" cost for many policies

Traditional long-term care insurance has a real downside that keeps some people from buying it: if you pay premiums for decades and never end up needing long-term care, that money is simply gone, the way term life insurance premiums are gone if you outlive the term. This has led to the rise of hybrid policies, which combine long-term care benefits with a life insurance or annuity component — if you never need long-term care, your beneficiaries still receive a death benefit, or you retain access to the underlying account value. Hybrid policies typically cost more upfront but address the "what if I never use it" concern directly.

Who should seriously consider it

  • People in their mid-50s to mid-60s with a family history suggesting elevated risk, or simply a desire to protect a meaningful amount of retirement savings from this specific risk
  • People without adult children or other family able to provide informal caregiving, who would otherwise need to fund all professional care out of pocket
  • Those with substantial assets they want to protect — Medicaid's asset spend-down requirement is a strong reason some people plan around it in advance with private insurance instead

Who might reasonably skip it

  • People with very limited assets, for whom Medicaid eligibility, while not ideal, may be a more realistic eventual outcome regardless of insurance
  • People with very substantial assets who can comfortably self-insure the cost of potential future care without meaningfully affecting their financial security
Long-term care insurance sits in an unusually narrow band of usefulness — most valuable for people who have meaningful assets to protect but aren't so wealthy that self-insuring the risk is trivial. It's worth an honest look at where your own situation falls on that spectrum, ideally well before your 60s.

A practical starting point

  1. If you're in your mid-50s to early 60s and healthy, get quotes now rather than waiting — the cost of waiting a decade is usually substantial
  2. Compare traditional and hybrid policy structures, since the "use it or lose it" tradeoff matters a lot to some people and not at all to others
  3. Talk to a fee-only financial advisor about how long-term care risk fits into your broader retirement plan, rather than treating it as an isolated purchase decision
  4. Discuss the topic openly with family — a plan that only exists in your own head doesn't help anyone make decisions if a health crisis happens suddenly

It's an uncomfortable topic to plan around, which is exactly why so many families end up making these decisions reactively during a crisis instead of proactively while there's still time and options on the table.
