Short-Term Health Insurance: Pros, Cons, and Hidden Risks

Dana Whitfield Personal Finance Writer Updated Aug 5, 2026 8 min read

Short-term health insurance ads are everywhere once you start looking for coverage — plans priced at a fraction of a marketplace premium, approved almost instantly, no long forms. For someone between jobs or waiting out an enrollment window, that price tag is tempting. It's also, in most cases, cheap for reasons that only become obvious after you actually need to use the plan.

That doesn't mean short-term plans are always a bad idea. It means they're a narrower tool than the advertising suggests, and knowing exactly what you're trading away for that lower premium matters.

What short-term health insurance actually is

Short-term, limited-duration insurance is exactly what it sounds like: temporary coverage, typically lasting anywhere from a month up to a year depending on your state (some states restrict short-term plans further, and a few don't allow them at all). Unlike ACA marketplace plans, short-term plans aren't required to comply with the Affordable Care Act's coverage rules — and that single fact explains almost every meaningful difference between the two.

The coverage gaps that catch people off guard

ProtectionACA Marketplace PlansShort-Term Plans
Pre-existing conditionsMust be covered, no exclusions or higher pricingOften excluded entirely or used to deny the application outright
Essential health benefits (maternity, mental health, prescriptions, etc.)Required to be coveredFrequently excluded or only partially covered
Annual/lifetime coverage capsNot allowedOften present — a serious illness can exceed the cap
Guaranteed renewalYes, annuallyNot guaranteed — insurer can decline to renew, especially after a claim
UnderwritingNone — can't be denied for health historyMedical underwriting is common — you can be turned down
The scenario that trips people up most: someone buys a short-term plan while healthy, develops a condition requiring ongoing treatment during the coverage period, and then discovers at renewal time that the plan won't renew — or renews but now excludes the condition that just emerged, since it's newly "pre-existing." This is the single biggest risk with short-term coverage, and it's rarely front and center in the marketing.

Who short-term plans genuinely make sense for

  • A narrow, defined coverage gap — for example, a few weeks between graduating college and a new job's benefits kicking in, where you're healthy and mainly want protection against a catastrophic, unlikely event.
  • Missing open enrollment without a qualifying life event — if you genuinely can't get an ACA plan until the next enrollment period and need some coverage in the meantime.
  • Budget-constrained, healthy individuals who understand and accept the tradeoffs, mainly want protection against a major accident or emergency, and don't have ongoing prescriptions or conditions that need consistent coverage.

Who should generally avoid them

  • Anyone with an existing health condition requiring ongoing care or medication — short-term plans are likely to exclude it, deny the application, or leave you exposed at renewal.
  • Anyone who might become pregnant during the coverage period — maternity care is rarely covered on short-term plans.
  • Anyone eligible for ACA subsidies — if you qualify for a meaningful subsidy, a marketplace plan can end up costing about the same or less than a short-term plan, while offering dramatically more protection.

What to actually check before buying one

  1. Read the exclusions list directly — not the marketing page, the actual policy document — for anything relevant to your situation.
  2. Check the maximum coverage period allowed in your state and whether the plan can be renewed, and under what conditions.
  3. Look for an annual or lifetime coverage maximum and consider whether that cap would actually protect you in a worst-case scenario like a major surgery or hospitalization.
  4. Confirm whether pre-existing conditions are excluded — including conditions you might not think to mention, since these plans often use broad definitions.
  5. Compare the total cost against a subsidized marketplace plan before assuming the short-term option is actually cheaper for your situation.
A short-term plan isn't "bad insurance" so much as it's a different product wearing a similar name — closer to a temporary safety net for catastrophic events than to comprehensive health coverage. Buying one without knowing that distinction is where people get burned.

A quick gut-check before you buy

Ask yourself honestly: if I got seriously sick or injured during this coverage period, would this plan actually cover most of the cost, or would I discover an exclusion right when I needed the coverage most? If you're not confident in the answer, it's worth spending twenty minutes reading the policy's exclusions section directly, or calling the insurer with specific questions, before signing up based on the price alone.

Short-term health insurance fills a real, narrow gap for a specific set of situations. The mistake isn't buying one — it's buying one without reading past the premium.
