Home Insurance and Natural Disasters: What's Excluded

Marcus Reyes Insurance & Risk Writer Updated Aug 6, 2026 8 min read

One of the most consistent and consequential misunderstandings in homeownership is assuming a standard homeowners policy covers "natural disasters" as a category. It doesn't — it covers a specific, named list of perils, and some of the most damaging and common disasters in the U.S. simply aren't on that list. Finding this out after a flood or an earthquake, rather than before, is one of the more expensive lessons a homeowner can learn.

What a standard homeowners policy typically does cover

Most standard policies cover damage from a defined set of named perils, which generally includes: fire, lightning, windstorms and hail, explosions, theft and vandalism, and weight of snow or ice on the structure. Some of these have limits or specific conditions attached, but broadly, this list is the baseline most homeowners can rely on.

What's almost always excluded — and needs a separate policy

Flood damage

This is the single biggest gap and the one that catches the most people off guard. Standard homeowners insurance excludes flood damage entirely, regardless of the cause — heavy rain, storm surge, overflowing rivers, or general water intrusion from outside the home. Flood coverage has to be purchased separately, typically through the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) or a private flood insurance policy. Many homeowners in FEMA-designated high-risk flood zones are required by their mortgage lender to carry it — but plenty of serious flood damage happens outside those officially mapped zones too, where coverage is optional but arguably just as important.

A common and costly misconception: water damage from a burst pipe inside your home is typically covered under a standard policy (it's considered "sudden and accidental" internal water damage). Water that enters from outside — a flooded street, a swollen creek, storm surge — is flood damage and is excluded unless you carry separate flood coverage. The source of the water, not just the fact that your home got wet, determines what's covered.

Earthquake damage

Standard policies exclude earthquake damage as well, and this needs its own separate policy or endorsement, commonly available in earthquake-prone states. Coverage typically includes structural damage and can extend to personal property, depending on the specific policy, and often comes with a notably higher deductible than standard homeowners coverage — sometimes calculated as a percentage of your home's value rather than a flat dollar amount.

Sewer and drain backup

Damage from a sewer line or drain backing up into your home is usually excluded from standard policies, but is commonly available as an inexpensive add-on endorsement. Given how disruptive and unpleasant this specific type of damage is, and how affordable the add-on typically is, it's one of the more commonly recommended additions for homeowners in older neighborhoods with aging sewer infrastructure.

Mold (with important nuance)

Mold resulting from a sudden, covered event — say, a burst pipe you addressed quickly — is often covered as part of the resulting cleanup. Mold that develops from ongoing, unaddressed moisture or humidity issues is typically excluded as a maintenance issue rather than a sudden loss. Many policies also cap mold remediation coverage at a specific dollar amount even when it is covered.

Landslides, mudslides, and earth movement

Generally excluded from both standard homeowners and even most earthquake policies, and typically requiring a specialized difference-in-conditions (DIC) policy in areas prone to this risk — often coastal hillside communities or areas with a history of unstable terrain.

Why this matters even if you don't live in an "obvious" risk zone

Flood risk in particular has been expanding beyond traditional FEMA flood maps as rainfall patterns and development change over time — a meaningful share of flood claims each year come from properties outside officially designated high-risk zones. "I'm not in a flood zone" is a weaker basis for skipping flood coverage than it used to be; it's worth checking current flood risk data for your specific address rather than relying on an outdated assumption.

How to actually check your exposure

  1. Pull up your specific address on FEMA's flood map service to check your current designated flood zone
  2. Ask your insurer directly which named perils your policy excludes — don't assume, since exclusions can vary somewhat by insurer and state
  3. If you're in an earthquake-prone region, get a quote for earthquake coverage even if you've never considered it — premiums vary more than people expect between providers
  4. Consider the sewer/drain backup endorsement if your home is older or in an area with aging infrastructure — it's usually inexpensive relative to the protection
  5. Re-check this list after any major home improvement, since renovations can sometimes change your coverage needs
The phrase "homeowners insurance" implies more comprehensive protection than most policies actually provide. Reading your policy's exclusions section once, rather than assuming, is the difference between a manageable claim and an uninsured disaster.

None of these additional policies are exotic or hard to obtain — they're just not automatic, and insurers have little incentive to proactively point out what your standard policy doesn't cover. That part is on you to check.
