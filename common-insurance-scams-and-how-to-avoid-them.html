Common Insurance Scams and How to Avoid Them

Dana Whitfield Personal Finance Writer Updated Aug 7, 2026 9 min read

Insurance fraud isn't just a "someone else's problem" issue that shows up in industry statistics — it directly costs policyholders money, both through higher premiums that fraud-driven claims push onto everyone, and through scams that target consumers directly, sometimes convincingly enough to cost real money on the spot. Knowing the specific, recurring patterns makes them dramatically easier to spot before they cost you anything.

Scams targeting consumers directly

Fake or unlicensed agents

Someone offers suspiciously cheap coverage, collects a premium payment, and either disappears or sells a policy that isn't actually backed by a real, licensed insurer. This is especially common in the direct-to-consumer digital ad space, where a fraudulent operation can look nearly identical to a legitimate agency.

The check that stops this cold: before paying any premium, verify the agent's license and the insurer's standing directly through your state's department of insurance website — every state maintains a public, searchable database. It takes about two minutes and eliminates this entire category of scam.

Post-disaster contractor fraud

After a major storm, flood, or other regional disaster, fraudulent "contractors" often go door to door offering fast repairs, sometimes claiming to already be working with your insurer or offering to "handle everything" including your claim. Red flags include demanding full payment upfront, pressuring you to sign an assignment of benefits (which can sign over your claim rights to them), or refusing to provide licensing and insurance documentation.

Phishing posing as your insurer

Emails or texts claiming to be from your insurance company, asking you to "verify" account details, click a link to "update payment information," or confirm personal information to "process a refund." Legitimate insurers generally don't ask for sensitive information this way — when in doubt, don't click the link; instead, call your insurer directly using the number on your actual policy documents or the back of your insurance card, not a number provided in the suspicious message.

Fake premium payment requests

A call or message claiming your policy is about to lapse unless you make an immediate payment, often with urgency-inducing pressure and an unusual payment method requested (gift cards, wire transfer, cryptocurrency). Legitimate insurers don't ask for payment via gift cards, and creating artificial urgency is a classic scam tactic across nearly every type of fraud, not just insurance.

Scams that raise costs for everyone, even if you're not directly targeted

Staged auto accidents

Organized fraud rings sometimes stage accidents — sudden, unnecessary hard braking to induce a rear-end collision, for example — specifically to file inflated injury and repair claims. If you're ever involved in a collision that feels suspicious (the other driver braked with no apparent reason, multiple "passengers" appear who weren't visible before the crash, or several unrelated parties immediately claim injuries), document everything thoroughly with photos and get an independent police report rather than settling informally at the scene.

Inflated or fabricated claims

Exaggerating the value of a legitimate claim, or filing a claim for damage that didn't actually occur or existed before the claimed incident, is a common form of fraud that ultimately drives premiums up industry-wide, since insurers price policies based partly on aggregate fraud losses across their entire book of business.

Most insurance fraud isn't dramatic or cinematic — it's a slightly inflated repair estimate, a slightly exaggerated injury claim, a slightly too-convenient staged accident. The aggregate cost of all these "slightly" fraudulent claims is what shows up in everyone else's premiums.

How to protect yourself as a policyholder

  1. Verify any agent or insurer through your state's department of insurance website before paying a premium or signing anything
  2. Never pay via gift card, wire transfer, or cryptocurrency for a legitimate insurance premium — these payment methods are used almost exclusively in scams
  3. Be cautious with post-disaster contractors — get multiple quotes, verify licensing, and never sign an assignment of benefits without fully understanding what it means
  4. Contact your insurer directly using the number on your policy documents if you get a suspicious call, text, or email, rather than responding through the channel the message came from
  5. Report suspected fraud to your state's insurance fraud bureau or department of insurance — most states have a dedicated reporting mechanism, and reporting helps limit the broader cost fraud imposes on all policyholders

A note on your own claims

It's worth saying directly: exaggerating your own legitimate claim, even a little, crosses into fraud too, and carries real legal and financial consequences beyond just a denied claim. The line between "advocating firmly for a fair settlement" and "committing fraud" is documentation and honesty — stick to what actually happened and what it actually costs, and you're protected; inflate it, and you're not.

Most consumers will never be targeted by an elaborate scam, but the basic verification habits — checking a license, never paying via gift card, calling your insurer directly instead of clicking a link — cost nothing and close off the entire category of risk.
