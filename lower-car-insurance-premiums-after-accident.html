How to Lower Car Insurance Premiums After an Accident

Marcus Reyes Insurance & Risk Writer Updated Aug 5, 2026 8 min read

The renewal notice after an at-fault accident is a specific kind of unpleasant surprise — the same coverage you had last year, suddenly costing 30, 40, sometimes 60% more. It feels arbitrary, but it isn't. Insurers price risk based on recent history, and an at-fault accident is one of the strongest signals they use. The good news is that the increase isn't permanent, and there's more you can do about it than just waiting it out.

First, understand how long the accident actually affects your rate

Most insurers look back three to five years of driving history when calculating your premium, and a single at-fault accident typically has the biggest impact on your rate in the first year or two after it happens, gradually tapering off as it ages out of the insurer's primary rating window. It's rarely a cliff — go from "very expensive" to "back to normal" overnight — but a gradual step down as the incident moves further into the past.

A distinction that matters: not every accident affects your rate the same way. A not-at-fault accident (someone rear-ends you at a red light) generally has little to no impact on your premium with most insurers, since you weren't the one deemed responsible. It's the at-fault determination specifically that drives the increase — always check your claim's fault determination if your rate jumps and you're not sure why.

What actually helps bring the rate back down

1. Ask about accident forgiveness — before or after the fact

Many insurers offer an accident forgiveness program, either as a standard perk after a set number of claim-free years, or as an add-on you can purchase. If you have it and qualify, your first at-fault accident may not raise your rate at all. If you don't have it yet, ask your insurer whether it's available for future accidents — worth doing regardless of whether you've had a recent claim.

2. Take a defensive driving course

A surprising number of states and insurers offer a rate discount for completing an approved defensive driving or accident prevention course, sometimes specifically available after an at-fault accident to help offset the increase. Courses are often just a few hours online and cost far less than the discount is typically worth over a year or two.

3. Shop around instead of assuming your current insurer is the only option

Insurers don't all weigh the same accident the same way. One company's algorithm might hit you with a 45% increase; another might apply a smaller bump, especially if you have other factors working in your favor — long tenure as a driver, other policies bundled, a clean record before this one incident. Getting quotes from three or four insurers after an accident, rather than passively accepting your current renewal, regularly turns up meaningful savings.

4. Raise your deductible

If cash flow allows, raising your collision and comprehensive deductible — say, from $500 to $1,000 — lowers your premium immediately, and can help offset some of the accident-related increase. Just make sure you'd actually be able to cover the higher deductible out of pocket if you needed to file another claim.

5. Review and trim other coverage that may no longer make sense

If your car is older or has dropped significantly in value, comprehensive and collision coverage might now cost more annually than the car itself is worth in a total-loss payout. This isn't specific to post-accident situations, but it's a good moment to reassess your overall coverage structure while you're already shopping around.

6. Ask about all available discounts again

Good student discounts, low-mileage discounts, multi-policy bundling, telematics/usage-based programs, paying in full rather than monthly — insurers have a long list of discounts that often aren't applied automatically. After a rate increase is exactly the right moment to call and ask, point-blank, "what discounts am I not currently getting that I might qualify for?"

The mistake most people make after a rate increase is assuming there's nothing to do but pay it. In reality, the post-accident renewal is one of the best times to actively shop and negotiate, because you have the clearest possible reason to compare options.

What not to do

Don't let coverage lapse to avoid a high premium — even a short gap in coverage can itself become a rating factor that raises future premiums across the board, on top of being illegal in most states if you're still driving. And don't assume switching insurers means starting over with no credit for a clean history before the accident — most insurers do consider your longer driving history, not just the single incident, when quoting a new policy.

A short action plan

  1. Confirm the accident was recorded as at-fault, not not-at-fault, since that changes the picture significantly
  2. Ask your current insurer about accident forgiveness and any unused discounts
  3. Take an approved defensive driving course if one's available in your state
  4. Get quotes from 3–4 other insurers rather than assuming your renewal price is fixed
  5. Reassess your deductible and coverage levels while you're already comparing options
  6. Revisit your rates again in a year or two as the accident ages out of the primary rating window

An accident-related rate hike stings, but it's a temporary, actively manageable cost — not a life sentence on your insurance bill.
