Travel Insurance for International Trips: What's Actually Covered

Dana Whitfield Personal Finance Writer Updated Aug 6, 2026 8 min read

Most U.S. health insurance plans quietly stop working the moment you leave the country — a detail plenty of travelers only discover after they're already standing in a foreign hospital, being asked how they intend to pay. Travel insurance exists to cover exactly that gap, along with a handful of other trip-specific risks that don't map neatly onto any coverage you already have. The confusing part is that "travel insurance" isn't one thing — it's usually a bundle of several distinct protections, and knowing what each piece actually does matters more than the total premium.

The core components of a standard travel insurance policy

Trip cancellation and interruption

Reimburses prepaid, non-refundable trip costs if you have to cancel before departure, or cut the trip short after it's already started, for a covered reason — typically illness, injury, a death in the family, or a handful of other specifically listed events. This is usually the single biggest dollar-value component for travelers who've prepaid a lot for flights, hotels, or tours.

Emergency medical coverage

Covers medical treatment abroad, which matters enormously given that most domestic U.S. health plans (including Medicare, notably) provide little to no coverage outside the country. Policy limits vary widely, so it's worth checking the actual coverage cap — a $50,000 medical limit sounds like a lot until you consider that a serious hospitalization abroad can exceed that quickly in some countries.

Emergency medical evacuation

This is a distinct coverage from basic medical treatment, and arguably one of the most financially important pieces of the whole policy. It covers the cost of transporting you to an adequate medical facility, or back to the U.S., in a serious emergency — and this can be genuinely expensive, sometimes reaching into six figures for an evacuation from a remote location requiring a medical flight. Confirm your policy's evacuation coverage limit specifically, separate from its general medical limit.

Why evacuation coverage deserves special attention: a medical evacuation from a remote or under-resourced area can cost far more than the underlying medical treatment itself. Travelers heading somewhere with limited local medical infrastructure — certain rural or high-altitude destinations, for instance — should specifically check this coverage limit rather than assuming a general "travel insurance" policy has it adequately covered.

Baggage loss, delay, and personal effects

Covers lost, stolen, or significantly delayed luggage, usually up to a set dollar limit with sub-limits for individual high-value items like electronics or jewelry. Useful, but generally the smallest-dollar-value component of a typical policy.

Travel delay

Reimburses reasonable additional expenses — meals, lodging, essential purchases — if your trip is delayed beyond a specified number of hours for a covered reason, like a missed connection or severe weather.

What's commonly excluded or requires an upgrade

SituationStandard policy status
Pre-existing medical conditionsOften excluded unless you buy within a specific window after your initial trip deposit (a "pre-existing condition waiver")
Cancelling simply because you changed your mindNot covered under standard "named reason" policies — requires Cancel For Any Reason (CFAR) coverage
Extreme sports / high-risk activitiesOften excluded or requires a specific adventure sports add-on
Travel to countries under a State Department travel advisoryCoverage can be limited or excluded for the highest-risk advisory levels — check before booking
Pandemic-related cancellationsCoverage varies significantly by policy and has changed considerably since 2020 — read this specific clause carefully

Do you actually need it?

The honest answer depends heavily on three things: how much of your trip is prepaid and non-refundable, whether your existing health insurance provides any coverage abroad, and your destination's healthcare costs and accessibility. A cheap, fully refundable weekend trip domestically has a very different risk profile than a $6,000 prepaid international trip to a destination with expensive private healthcare and limited public options for foreign visitors.

  • Check your existing health insurance first — some plans offer limited emergency coverage abroad, though usually far less comprehensive than domestic coverage, and evacuation coverage is almost never included.
  • Check your credit card's travel benefits — some premium travel credit cards include baggage or trip delay coverage automatically, which might reduce how much additional coverage you need to buy.
  • Weigh the actual dollar amount at risk — the more you've prepaid and the more remote or expensive your destination's healthcare system, the stronger the case for a comprehensive policy.

How to actually compare policies

  1. Compare the medical and evacuation coverage limits specifically — these matter most for international trips and vary a lot between policies
  2. Read the list of "covered reasons" for cancellation carefully — a policy that only covers a narrow list of reasons is meaningfully different from one with broader coverage
  3. Check whether pre-existing conditions apply to you, and whether a waiver is available and worth buying within the eligibility window
  4. Confirm the policy covers your specific destination, including any high-risk activities you're planning
  5. Compare total premium against the total prepaid, non-refundable cost of your trip — as a rough guide, travel insurance often runs somewhere around 4–10% of total trip cost, though this varies with age, trip length, and coverage level
Travel insurance is really several different products stitched together under one name. The right approach isn't asking "should I buy travel insurance" in the abstract — it's asking which specific pieces of that bundle actually protect against real risk for this particular trip.

For a domestic weekend trip with fully refundable bookings, the case for travel insurance is genuinely weak. For an international trip with significant prepaid costs and limited existing medical coverage abroad, it's one of the more clearly worthwhile purchases in the entire trip-planning process.
