Renters Insurance: What It Actually Covers That Landlords Won't

Dana Whitfield Personal Finance Writer Updated Aug 6, 2026 8 min read

A pipe burst in my upstairs neighbor's unit a few years back and flooded straight through my apartment ceiling. My landlord's insurance repaired the drywall, the flooring, the ceiling — the building. It did not replace my laptop, my mattress, or the box of books that turned into pulp on the closet floor. That's the gap renters insurance exists to close, and it surprises a lot of first-time renters the hard way.

The core misunderstanding: whose insurance covers what

A landlord's policy — usually a dwelling or commercial property policy — covers the physical structure: the building itself, its systems, and often common areas. It has no obligation to cover anything you own inside your unit, and in most cases, it has no obligation to protect you if someone gets hurt in your apartment and decides to sue. Those two gaps — your belongings, and your personal liability — are exactly what renters insurance is built to fill.

What a standard renters policy actually includes

1. Personal property coverage

This pays to repair or replace your belongings — furniture, electronics, clothing, kitchenware — if they're damaged or destroyed by a covered event: fire, certain water damage, theft, vandalism, and a handful of other named perils depending on the policy. It typically applies whether the loss happens inside your apartment or, with some limits, outside it too — for example, if your bag is stolen while you're out.

2. Liability coverage

If someone is injured in your apartment and you're found legally responsible — a guest slips on a wet floor, for instance — liability coverage pays for their medical costs and your legal defense, up to your policy's limit. This also typically extends to situations where you accidentally cause damage to someone else's property, not just injuries.

3. Additional living expenses (loss of use)

If your apartment becomes temporarily uninhabitable due to a covered event — a fire, for example — this pays for reasonable costs of living elsewhere: a hotel, restaurant meals beyond your normal grocery budget, and similar temporary expenses while your unit is repaired.

4. Medical payments to others

A smaller, no-fault coverage that pays modest medical bills for a guest injured in your home, regardless of whether you were legally at fault — useful for handling minor incidents quickly without a liability claim.

What it typically doesn't cover: standard renters policies usually exclude flood and earthquake damage (available as separate policies or endorsements in higher-risk areas), and often cap coverage for high-value items like jewelry, art, or expensive electronics unless you add a specific rider or endorsement for them.

What it actually costs

This is the detail that surprises most renters: coverage is genuinely inexpensive relative to what it protects. A typical renters policy runs somewhere in the range of $15–$30 a month for a reasonable amount of personal property and liability coverage, varying by location, coverage amount, and deductible. For most renters, that's less than a single streaming subscription — a small, predictable cost against a real, if infrequent, risk.

How much coverage do you actually need?

The most useful exercise is a rough home inventory: walk through your apartment and estimate the replacement cost — not the used, depreciated value — of your furniture, electronics, clothing, and other belongings. Most people are surprised how quickly this adds up once they account for everything, not just the big-ticket items.

Living situationTypical starting coverage amount
Studio or 1-bedroom, minimal furniture$15,000–$25,000
2-3 bedroom, furnished, some electronics$30,000–$50,000
Larger household, higher-value belongings$50,000+, often with specific riders for valuables

Replacement cost vs. actual cash value — read this before choosing

Policies typically offer two different ways to value a claim: replacement cost, which pays what it actually costs to buy a new equivalent item today, and actual cash value, which factors in depreciation — meaning a five-year-old laptop gets valued at what a five-year-old laptop is worth now, not what a new one costs. Replacement cost coverage costs somewhat more but is almost always worth the difference, since actual cash value payouts on older electronics or furniture can be disappointingly small.

Why landlords increasingly require it

Many landlords and property managers now require proof of renters insurance as a lease condition, partly because it protects them too — if you accidentally cause damage (a kitchen fire, an overflowing bathtub), your liability coverage can pay for the resulting damage to the building, rather than that cost falling entirely on the landlord's policy or becoming a dispute between you and them.

Renters insurance is one of the highest value-for-cost products in personal insurance — a genuinely small monthly cost protecting against a loss that, for most renters, would otherwise mean starting over from zero.

Getting started

  1. Do a quick walkthrough inventory of your belongings, with rough replacement values, and take photos or a video for your records
  2. Decide on a liability limit — many people default to $100,000, though higher limits cost relatively little extra
  3. Choose replacement cost coverage over actual cash value if the price difference is reasonable
  4. Ask about riders for any high-value items — jewelry, musical instruments, expensive electronics — that might exceed standard sub-limits
  5. Check whether bundling with an existing auto policy offers a discount, since many insurers offer one

It's a policy people tend to buy the week after something goes wrong, not before. The whole point is getting it in place before that week happens.
