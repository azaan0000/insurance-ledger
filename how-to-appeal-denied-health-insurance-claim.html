How to Appeal a Denied Health Insurance Claim

Marcus Reyes Insurance & Risk Writer Updated Aug 5, 2026 9 min read

A denial letter is designed to sound final. "Claim denied." "Not medically necessary." "Service not covered under your plan." The tone is bureaucratic and absolute, and it's easy to read one and assume there's nothing left to do except pay the bill. That assumption costs people money they didn't actually have to spend — a meaningful share of denied claims that get formally appealed end up at least partially overturned, and most people never file the appeal in the first place.

Here's how the process actually works, and how to give your appeal the best odds.

First, understand why claims actually get denied

Denials generally fall into a few recurring buckets, and knowing which one you're dealing with changes your entire appeal strategy:

  • Administrative errors — a wrong billing code, a missing pre-authorization number, a typo in your member ID. These are often the easiest to fix and sometimes don't even require a formal appeal, just a corrected resubmission.
  • "Not medically necessary" determinations — the insurer's clinical reviewer disagreed with your provider about whether the service was needed. This is where a strong appeal with supporting documentation from your doctor matters most.
  • Out-of-network denials — the provider wasn't in your plan's network, sometimes without your knowledge (this happens more than people expect with hospital-based specialists like anesthesiologists or radiologists).
  • Coverage exclusions — the service genuinely isn't covered under your specific plan. These are the hardest to overturn, though not always impossible if the service can be reframed under a covered category.
Read the denial letter closely before doing anything else. It's required by law to include the specific reason for denial and instructions for appealing. That reason is your roadmap — an appeal addressing a billing code error looks completely different from one addressing a medical necessity dispute.

The appeals process, step by step

Step 1: Request the full claim file

You're entitled to request the complete file the insurer used to make its decision, including any clinical guidelines cited. This document often reveals exactly what the reviewer was looking at — and sometimes reveals that they were working from incomplete medical records, which becomes the core of your appeal.

Step 2: File an internal appeal

This goes back to the same insurance company, but to a different reviewer than the one who made the original decision. Internal appeals typically need to be filed within 180 days of the denial, though check your specific plan's deadline. Include:

  • A clear, calm letter stating what you're appealing and why the denial was wrong
  • A letter of medical necessity from your treating physician, addressing the specific reason cited in the denial
  • Any relevant medical records, test results, or clinical guidelines supporting the treatment
  • A copy of the original denial letter and your policy's relevant coverage language

Step 3: If denied again, request an external review

If the internal appeal is also denied, you generally have the right to an independent external review — a party outside the insurance company, often a medical professional in the relevant specialty, reviews the case fresh. External review decisions are typically binding on the insurer, meaning if the external reviewer sides with you, the insurer has to pay. This step exists specifically because internal appeals can feel like asking the fox to re-guard the henhouse — external review adds a genuinely independent check.

What actually makes an appeal succeed

The single biggest factor is specificity. A letter that says "please reconsider, this was an important treatment" rarely moves the needle. A letter that says "the denial cites lack of medical necessity under [specific clinical guideline], but the attached letter from Dr. [Name] and the enclosed test results directly address that criterion" gives the reviewer something concrete to act on.

Insurance reviewers are working from a file, not from your actual medical situation. Your job in an appeal is to make sure the file tells the full story — because the original denial was almost certainly based on an incomplete one.

Don't underestimate your doctor's office

Provider offices deal with claim denials constantly, and many have a staff member — sometimes in billing, sometimes a dedicated appeals coordinator — who has written dozens of successful appeal letters for the exact same denial reason you're facing. Ask directly whether they can help draft or support your appeal; this is a normal, common request and most offices are willing to do it, partly because they want to get paid too.

Timing matters more than people think

Denial letters include a specific appeal deadline, and missing it can mean losing your right to appeal entirely, regardless of how strong your case is. Set a calendar reminder the day you receive a denial, and don't wait until close to the deadline to start gathering documentation — medical records requests alone can take a couple of weeks to fulfill.

For urgent or ongoing treatment: ask about expedited appeals

If a denial affects care you need urgently — an ongoing treatment, a time-sensitive procedure — ask specifically about an expedited appeal. Most plans are required to offer a faster review process for cases where a standard timeline could seriously jeopardize your health, sometimes turning around a decision within 72 hours instead of weeks.

A simple appeal checklist

  1. Read the denial letter carefully and identify the exact reason cited
  2. Request your complete claim file from the insurer
  3. Contact your provider's office about supporting documentation and a letter of medical necessity
  4. Write a clear, specific appeal letter addressing the exact denial reason
  5. Submit before the deadline, and keep copies of everything
  6. If denied again, file for external review — don't assume the internal denial is final

A denial letter is a starting position, not a verdict. It's written that way on purpose, and it works on people who don't know an appeals process exists. Now you do.
