How Home Insurance Claims Affect Future Premiums

Dana Whitfield Personal Finance Writer Updated Aug 6, 2026 7 min read

Home insurance can feel like a use-it-or-lose-it benefit — you pay the premium every year, so when something breaks, filing a claim feels like the obvious move. It isn't always. Unlike some other types of insurance, home insurance claims can follow you for years through a system most homeowners have never heard of, and a small claim can end up costing more in future premium increases than it saved you on the original repair.

Meet CLUE — the report that follows your house and you

Most insurers report claims to the Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange (CLUE), a database that tracks claims history tied to both a property and, to an extent, the person insuring it. When you apply for a new policy — whether you're switching insurers or buying a new home — the new insurer typically pulls your CLUE report as part of underwriting. A history of multiple claims, even relatively small ones, can result in higher quotes or, in some cases, difficulty getting coverage at all.

The detail that surprises people: CLUE claims history generally stays on the report for about seven years. That's a long window for a single water-damage claim from a slow leak to keep quietly nudging your premium up, or to affect your ability to get a competitive quote when you're shopping around.

Not all claims affect your rate equally

Claim typeTypical premium impact
Water damage (non-flood, e.g. burst pipe)Often one of the more heavily weighted claim types — statistically linked to repeat claims and higher future risk
FireSignificant impact, though usually a rare, one-time event that insurers weigh differently than repeat-risk claims like water damage
Liability (e.g. dog bite, slip and fall)Meaningful impact, particularly if it results in a larger payout
Wind/hail damage (in wind-prone regions)Often weighted less heavily since it's a regional risk factor rather than a property-specific one
TheftModerate impact, generally treated as lower-risk-of-repeat than water damage claims

The math on small claims

This is where the "should I even file this" decision actually gets made. Say you have a $1,000 deductible and a repair estimate of $1,800 for some minor water damage. Filing the claim nets you $800 after your deductible. But if that claim contributes to a rate increase of, say, $30 a month for the next several years, you've paid back that $800 "savings" within about two years — and kept paying extra well beyond that, on top of now having a claims history that could affect future shopping.

A rough rule of thumb: for a repair cost that's under 2–3 times your deductible, it's often worth getting a repair quote and doing the premium-impact math before filing, rather than filing automatically. For anything at or above your policy's coverage threshold for a genuinely significant loss — major storm damage, a fire, a large water damage event — filing is usually the right call regardless of the rate impact, since the alternative is paying the full cost yourself.

How to actually estimate the premium impact before filing

  1. Call your insurer or agent directly and ask how a claim of this type and size typically affects renewal pricing — many will give you a general sense even before you file.
  2. Ask whether you have claim-free discounts or a forgiveness benefit that would be lost by filing, since some insurers offer a "first claim forgiveness" type program similar to what's common in auto insurance.
  3. Get an actual repair estimate rather than guessing, so you're comparing real numbers against your deductible.
  4. Consider your broader claims history — a second or third claim in a short window is weighted more heavily than an isolated first claim, so your specific history matters as much as the size of this individual claim.

When filing is clearly the right call, regardless of rate impact

  • The damage significantly exceeds your deductible and represents a real financial hardship to pay out of pocket
  • The damage involves structural issues that could worsen without prompt, fully-funded repair
  • A liability situation is involved — a guest was injured, or you damaged someone else's property — where legal exposure matters more than the premium math
Insurance exists to protect against losses you genuinely can't absorb — not as a rebate program for every repair that happens to be covered. The best claims decisions treat the deductible math and the future premium impact as part of the same calculation, not two separate questions.

What to do instead of filing for smaller repairs

For damage that falls below the "clearly worth filing" threshold, paying out of pocket and keeping your claims history clean often works out cheaper over a multi-year horizon. It's also worth building a small home maintenance fund specifically for this category of repair — the kind of thing that's inconvenient but not devastating — so the decision isn't forced by not having the cash on hand.

None of this means avoid filing claims altogether — it means treat each one as a real financial decision, not an automatic reflex, since the true cost of a claim extends well past the day it's paid out.
