How Insurance Companies Calculate Your Premium

Marcus Reyes Insurance & Risk Writer Updated Aug 7, 2026 9 min read

Insurance pricing can feel arbitrary from the outside — two neighbors with similar cars pay noticeably different premiums, a rate jumps for no reason you can identify, a quote from one company is half of another's for what looks like identical coverage. It isn't arbitrary. It's the output of a detailed actuarial rating formula, built from historical claims data across millions of policyholders, applied to your specific profile. Understanding the actual inputs demystifies a lot of what otherwise feels like a black box.

The core principle behind all insurance pricing

Insurers price premiums based on risk pooling and statistical prediction: they look at large datasets of past claims to estimate how likely a given type of policyholder is to file a claim, and how expensive that claim is likely to be, then price accordingly with a margin for their own operating costs and profit. Every rating factor exists because, across large enough datasets, it's been shown to correlate with claim frequency or claim severity — even factors that don't feel intuitively related to risk on the surface.

Common rating factors across insurance types

Location

Where you live affects pricing across nearly every insurance category — auto insurance considers local accident rates, theft rates, and even weather patterns; home insurance considers local natural disaster risk, crime statistics, and the cost of local construction and labor for repairs; health insurance considers regional healthcare costs. This is one of the most heavily weighted factors across the board.

Age and experience

Younger, less experienced drivers statistically file more claims, which is why auto insurance for a 19-year-old typically costs dramatically more than for a 45-year-old with an otherwise similar profile. Life insurance works in the opposite pricing direction relative to mortality risk — premiums rise with age since the statistical likelihood of a claim increases over time.

Claims history

Past claims — yours personally, and sometimes the property's history independent of who owns it — are one of the strongest predictors insurers use, since a history of claims correlates with future claims across large datasets, even when any individual claim might have been a one-off event.

Credit-based insurance scores

In most states, auto and home insurers are permitted to use a credit-based insurance score (different from your standard credit score, though built from similar underlying data) as a rating factor. Studies insurers rely on have shown a statistical correlation between certain credit behaviors and claims frequency, though this practice is controversial and outright prohibited in a handful of states.

Coverage amount and deductible

Higher coverage limits and lower deductibles both increase your premium, straightforwardly, since the insurer is taking on more financial exposure per claim.

An important legal note: insurers are prohibited from using certain factors in most or all states — race, religion, and national origin, most notably — regardless of any statistical correlation that might otherwise exist. Rating factors are also regulated and filed with state insurance departments, meaning insurers can't use just any variable they want; approved rating factors vary somewhat by state.

Factors you can actually influence

Not every rating factor is fixed. Several respond directly to choices you can make:

  • Deductible level — raising it lowers your premium, if you can comfortably cover the higher out-of-pocket cost in a claim
  • Coverage amount — right-sizing coverage to your actual needs, rather than over-insuring, controls cost directly
  • Bundling — combining policies with one insurer often unlocks a multi-policy discount
  • Available discounts — good student, low mileage, safety features, defensive driving courses, claims-free history, and paying in full rather than monthly all commonly reduce premiums, though insurers don't always apply them automatically
  • Improving your credit (where credit-based scoring applies) — over time, this can meaningfully affect auto and home premiums in states that permit the practice
  • Health habits — for life and health insurance specifically, quitting smoking and maintaining a healthy weight can shift your underwriting class significantly

Factors you generally can't influence

  • Your age (obviously) and, for life/health insurance, your existing health history
  • Your location, short of actually moving
  • Broad market trends — inflation in repair and medical costs, increased frequency of severe weather events, and rising litigation costs all push premiums up industry-wide, independent of anything specific to you
A premium isn't a judgment about you personally — it's a statistical estimate applied to a group of similar policyholders. Understanding the inputs won't necessarily lower your bill, but it does explain why two seemingly similar people can get very different quotes, and which levers are actually yours to pull.

Why shopping around still matters despite all this

Even though the underlying rating factors are broadly similar across insurers, each company weighs them differently in its own proprietary formula, and some specialize in certain risk profiles more competitively than others. This is exactly why getting multiple quotes regularly turns up meaningful differences for the same person and the same coverage — the inputs are similar, but the formulas aren't identical.

Understanding how premiums get built doesn't make insurance cheap, but it does turn a frustrating black box into a set of specific, mostly explainable factors — several of which are actually within your control.
